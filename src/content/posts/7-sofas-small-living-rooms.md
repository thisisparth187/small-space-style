A small living room forgives almost nothing. A sofa that is 6 inches too wide eats the walkway, and one that cannot get through a 30-inch door is a return, not a bargain. So this guide does one thing: sofas sized for real small living rooms, with exact widths in inches. Every pick below is under 84 inches wide, and most are under 76.

## How to measure before you shop

Do this before you click anything. It takes ten minutes and saves a return.

1. **Measure the wall the sofa goes on**, in inches. Then subtract 36 inches — that is the walkway you need in front of it, and the space for a side table or a lamp.
2. **Measure your narrowest doorway and stairwell turn.** A sofa's *packaged* width is not the width that matters; the diagonal depth — the height of the back plus the depth of the seat — is what has to clear the door frame.
3. **Measure your seat depth need.** Sit on a chair you like. Measure from the back of your hips to the back of your knees. That number, minus 2 inches, is your ideal seat depth. Most small-space sofas are 20–22 inches deep in the seat.
4. **Leave 18 inches between the sofa and the coffee table.** Less than that and you will bruise your shins. More than 24 inches and reaching your drink becomes a stretch.

A good rule for a room under 12 ft wide: a sofa between 68 and 78 inches wide, with a seat depth of 20–22 inches, and arms no wider than 5 inches each. Wide, rolled arms look lovely in a showroom. In a small room they steal 8–10 inches of sitting space.

## 1. The 72-inch apartment sofa — the hero pick

This is the pick for most readers. At 72 inches wide it seats three adults without crowding, fits a 10–12 ft wall with room for a side table, and clears most apartment doors when carried in on its side.

Look for: a hardwood frame, removable seat cushions, and arms under 5 inches wide. Avoid: a seat depth over 23 inches — on a 72-inch sofa, deep seats push the back cushions so far forward that the sofa feels like a daybed.

**Example price range to verify:** roughly $500–$900 for a well-made version in a standard fabric. If you see one under $300, check the frame material — a lot of very cheap sofas use softwood or particleboard frames that loosen within a year.

## 3. The budget 76-inch sofa

The cheapest way to seat three people. At 76 inches it is the widest pick here, so measure twice — it needs a wall of at least 9 feet once you leave space at the ends.

Budget sofas are usually firmer than expensive ones, because the foam is denser and cheaper. That is not always bad: a firm sofa holds its shape longer. But if you like sinking in, this is not your pick, and no throw pillow will fix it.

**Example price range to verify:** roughly $300–$600.

## 4. The 70-inch sleeper sofa

A sleeper makes sense in exactly two situations: a studio apartment, or a living room that regularly hosts overnight guests. Everywhere else it is a worse sofa that costs more — the folding mechanism adds weight, and the mattress fold creates a hard bar across the middle of the seat.

If you do buy one, check the return policy carefully. A 4-inch mattress is the minimum for an adult to sleep on without waking up sore. And measure the *open* length: a 70-inch sofa opens to a bed roughly 70 inches long, which is short for anyone over 5 ft 10 in.

**Example price range to verify:** roughly $600–$1,100.

## 5. The splurge 78-inch linen sofa — the splurge pick

This is the one to buy if the room is the first thing guests see and you want it to look considered. Linen-look fabric, slim track arms, and a 78-inch width that reads as a proper three-seater rather than an apartment compromise.

It is also the highest-maintenance pick here. Light linen shows every mark, and real linen wrinkles. If you have a dog that sheds dark fur or a toddler with jam hands, buy pick 7 instead and spend the difference on a rug.

Look for: performance or stain-treated linen blends rather than pure linen, and cushion covers you can remove.

**Example price range to verify:** roughly $1,000–$1,800.

## 6. The 66-inch armless sofa

The narrowest pick, and the easiest to get into a difficult apartment. No arms means nothing to catch on a door frame, and 66 inches wide means it fits walls that nothing else will.

The trade-off is real: there is nothing to lean on, and nothing to push against when you stand up. Older guests and anyone with knee trouble will notice. It works best paired with a good side table and a floor lamp.

**Example price range to verify:** roughly $400–$800.

## 7. The 74-inch washable-cover sofa

If you have kids, pets, or a habit of eating on the sofa, this is the only pick that makes sense long term. A washable cover turns a red-wine disaster into a laundry cycle instead of a new sofa.

Check two things before buying: that *all* the covers come off, not just the seat cushions, and that the covers are machine-washable, not dry-clean-only.

**Example price range to verify:** roughly $600–$1,200.

## What to check before you order

- **Overall width, in inches, from the retailer listing** — not from a review, not from memory.
- **Doorway and stairwell clearance** — the diagonal depth must clear your narrowest turn.
- **Seat depth and seat height** — 20–22 inches deep, 17–19 inches high suits most adults.
- **Frame and cushion details** — hardwood frame, removable covers, and a stated warranty.
- **Return policy and delivery** — who pays return shipping on a 90-pound sofa? Read it before you order, not after.
- **The price on the retailer page** — that is the real price. Any price quoted in this guide is an example range only.

## The short version

If you only remember one thing: **buy the 72-inch apartment sofa (pick 1) unless you have a specific reason not to.** It fits most small rooms, seats three, and clears most doors. Go narrower (pick 2 or 6) if your room is under 11 feet wide. Go washable (pick 7) if you have kids or pets. Go sleeper (pick 4) only if guests actually sleep over. And whatever you buy, measure first — the tape measure is free, the return is not.
